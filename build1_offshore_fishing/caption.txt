Find the weed line, find the mahi. Here's how offshore anglers troll a line of floating sargassum, what happens at the strike, and how to let the fish go in good shape.

Why the weed line:
• Mahi gather near floating things. NOAA Fisheries notes that females and smaller males are commonly found near natural and artificial floating objects, including floating Sargassum.
• The weed holds far more small fish than open water. In a Gulf Stream survey off North Carolina, Sargassum samples held 18,799 fish of at least 80 species, against 2,706 fish of 60 species in open water samples. Most were juveniles.
• Mahi eat what lives there. In 2,632 stomachs from the southeastern and Gulf coasts of the United States, dolphin were found feeding at the surface on fishes, crustaceans, insects and plants associated with floating Sargassum. A North Atlantic study found flyingfish, swimming crabs and other dolphinfish among their main prey.

The spread (angler technique, not research):
• Outriggers spread the lines wide: two baits ride out on the riggers and two close on the flat lines.
• Rigged ballyhoo and skirted lures skip on top and dive, trailing bubbles.

The strike (angler technique, not research):
• When a fish hits, the line pops free of the release clip and you fight it straight off the rod tip.
• That moment of slack gives the fish time to grab the bait and turn it before swallowing.
• Set your strike drag with a scale. A common rule: about 20 percent of line strength for mono up to 20 lb test, 25 percent for 30 to 50 lb mono, and 30 percent for 80 to 130 lb mono.

Release it right (NOAA Fisheries best practices):
• Unhook the fish in the water whenever you can.
• If it has to come out, aim for less than 60 seconds.
• Handle it as little as possible, with wet hands, a wet soft knotless or rubber net, and a dehooker ready.
• Bag and size limits differ by region and change over time, so check your local rules before you keep one.

Find the weed line. Find the mahi. Let it go right.
