# Sources for the record marlin masterclass

Every on-screen fact and every claim in the voiceover and caption, with where it comes from. Lines marked "angler
technique" are common angling advice, not research. The marlin (modelled about 4.9 m with the bill, illustrative and
not the record fish to scale), the boat (about 43 ft), the drop off and its depths (the cross section is not to
scale), the upwelling streaks (drawn), the spread layout, the strike and the tail walk are simulated and illustrative.

Note on access: the pages below were read through web search results (excerpts) on 27 October 2026; the notes
paraphrase those excerpts. Two lines of the brief's script were changed because the sources did not support them:
"landing the ultimate ocean record" (the record is the Pacific blue marlin all tackle record; the Atlantic blue marlin
record is heavier) and "upwellings force tuna and squid" (the diet sources name tuna first; squid appears only in an
Australian study).

## Beat 1: the record (hook)
- "In 1982, Jay de Beaubien hooked a 1,376 pound Pacific blue marlin off Kona" / "IGFA ALL TACKLE RECORD, MAY 31,
  1982": Marlin Magazine and Outdoor Life report the IGFA all tackle record Pacific blue marlin, 1,376 lb, caught by
  Jay de Beaubien on 31 May 1982 off Kona, Hawaii, aboard No Problem (Capt. Bobby Brown); 193 in long, 82 in girth.
  https://www.marlinmag.com/all-tackle-world-record-pacific-blue-marlin
  https://www.outdoorlife.com/fishing/world-record-marlin-igfa/
  The newest source found that says the record still stands is from 2021; its status in 2026 was not confirmed
  against the IGFA records search.
- "landed in under an hour" / "PACIFIC BLUE MARLIN · MARLIN MAGAZINE": Marlin Magazine gives about 40 minutes in one
  account and "less than one hour" or "one hour" in others. "Under an hour" holds for every account found except
  the one that says "one hour", so treat it as approximate.
  https://www.marlinmag.com/marlin-world-record
- The Atlantic blue marlin all tackle record is heavier (1,402 lb 2 oz, Paulo Amorim, Brazil, 1992; reported in the same search results),
  which is why the film says "Pacific blue marlin" and not "ocean record".
- Pins "trolled resin lure", "pacific blue marlin": labels of the simulated scene. The record fish took a silver and
  blue Kita lure (Marlin Magazine); the drawn lure is generic.

## Beat 2: the drop off, badge [1] THE DROP OFF LEDGE
- "Grander marlin hunt the ledges" (angler technique): Kona guides describe currents pushing onto the ledges, which
  gather baitfish and pelagic shrimp and draw marlin, tuna and other pelagics; "the Grounds" north of Honokohau are
  named. https://fishtrack.com/article/mechanics-of-the-kona-coastline
  https://inthespread.com/blog/marlin-fishing-kevin-hibbard-206
- "Upwellings push bait and small tuna up" / "KONA LEDGES: FISHTRACK · DIET: BROCK 1984": the ledge currents (above);
  and a study of 87 blue marlin caught in Hawaiian waters found small tunas were the single most important part of
  their diet. Brock RE (1984) A contribution to the trophic biology of the blue marlin (Makaira nigricans) in
  Hawaii. Pacific Science 38(2): 141 to 149.
  https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/76dfd5dc-c092-413e-9c03-8539ab755491
  NOAA's species profile also gives tuna and other open ocean fish as the main diet.
  https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/pacific-blue-marlin
- "grander": common angler usage for a blue marlin of about 1,000 lb or more (not a formal definition).
- Pins "steep volcanic drop off", "upwelling", "bait and small tuna": labels of the drawn diagram. The depths and the
  slope are not to scale. "DIAGRAM NOT TO SCALE" is on screen.

## Beat 3: the spread, badge [2] THE TROLLING SPREAD
- "A staggered four lure spread" / pins "short corner", "long corner", "short rigger", "long rigger" (angler
  technique): big game lure spreads are commonly set from the outriggers and from flat lines near the transom,
  staggered at different distances; one Kona charter describes four to six lines at different depths and distances,
  and a Kona captain says most Kona captains prefer a spread of artificial lures. The position names follow common
  usage; the distances drawn are illustrative.
  https://inthespread.com/blog/marlin-lure-spread
- "Troll at 7 to 9 knots" (angler technique): sources give 7 to 10 knots for big skirted lures and 8 to 9 knots as
  more likely to draw a blue marlin attack with artificials. The boat in the film runs at 8 knots
  (8 x 0.5144 = 4.12 m/s). https://fishgame.com/2021/04/targeting-blue-marlin/
  https://thetackleroom.com/blogs/news/trolling-speed-chart-optimal-speeds-by-species-and-lure-type
- "Resin heads trail smoke" / "heavy smoke trails that bring giants up" (angler technique): a pro captain's rule is
  "pop, smoke, pop, smoke"; marlin prefer active lures that smoke and splash. That lures "bring giants up from the
  depths" is the script's wording, not a measured claim. https://www.sportfishingmag.com/trolling-secrets-pros/
- Pin "smoke trail": the drawn bubble trail.

## Beat 4: the drag, badge [3] THE DRAG
- "40 lb of drag on 130 lb tackle" / "About 30 percent of line strength" / "STRIKE DRAG RULE: MARLIN MAGAZINE"
  (angler technique): Marlin Magazine's rule of thumb is a strike drag of about 30 percent of the line's breaking
  strength, "nearly 40 pounds" on 130 lb line (0.30 x 130 = 39 lb). Other guides use 25 to 30 percent or one third
  (about 33 to 43 lb on a 130). https://www.marlinmag.com/howto/using-drag-effectively-to-win-the-blue-marlin-fight
  https://www.pakula.com.au/index.php/btl/0001-between-the-lines-7/setting-the-drag
- "set massive double hooks into its hard jaw": script wording (angler technique); double hook rigs are common on
  big game lures, not measured here.
- Pins "line pouring off", "strike", "tail walk": labels of the simulated reel (a generic gold lever drag reel with
  a strike stop on its quadrant) and of the simulated jump.

## Outro
- "Master the spread, fish the ledges, and you'll see why...": script wording summarising the beats above.

## What is simulated
The whole film is computer generated: the marlin, its lit up bars, the lures and their bubble trails, the boat, the
reel, the sea, the drop off diagram, the upwelling streaks, the strike and the tail walk. It is not footage of
de Beaubien's fish or of any real catch.

## Caption extras
- "keep it in the water and revive it before you let it go (angler practice)": common catch and release practice,
  given as advice, not as a measured claim.
- "the ultimate big game fish": script and caption wording, not a ranking.
