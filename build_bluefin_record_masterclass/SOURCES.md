# Sources for the bluefin record masterclass

Every on-screen fact and every claim in the voiceover and caption, with where it comes from. Lines marked "angler
technique" are common angling advice, not research. The fish (modelled at 2.85 m), the boat, the depths in the cross
section (the thermocline is drawn at 28 m and the diagram is not to scale), the bioluminescent trails (stylised), the
temperature break, the skipping bait and the fight are simulated and illustrative.

Note on access: the pages below were read through web search results (excerpts) on 23 October 2026; direct page
fetches from this build machine failed, so the notes below paraphrase those excerpts.

## Beat 1: the record (hook)
- "In 1979, Ken Fraser landed a 1,496 pound bluefin" / "FRASER'S RECORD FISH, OCTOBER 26, 1979": Outdoor Life and
  Saltwater Sportsman report that Ken Fraser caught a 1,496 lb bluefin on 26 October 1979 off Aulds Cove, Nova
  Scotia, and that it is the IGFA all tackle world record.
  https://www.outdoorlife.com/fishing/world-record-tuna/
  https://www.saltwatersportsman.com/?p=52293
- "A record untouched for over 40 years" / "IGFA ALL TACKLE WORLD RECORD": same sources (still listed as the all
  tackle record). 1979 to 2026 is 47 years, so "over 40" holds. The IGFA record entry itself was not read directly.
- "128.5 in, fraser's fish" pin: Outdoor Life gives the fish as 128.5 in long with a 99 in girth. The pin points at
  the modelled fish, which is 2.85 m (112 in): the model is illustrative and not Fraser's fish to scale.
- Fight length is not stated anywhere in the film: sources disagree (45 minutes, just under an hour, most of a day).
- Marlin Magazine reports that Ken Fraser died on 24 October 2025, aged 81.
  https://www.marlinmag.com/travel/remembering-ken-fraser/

## Beat 2: the gear, badge [1] HEAVY TROLLING RIG
- "130 lb class line on a 14/0 reel" / "FRASER'S TACKLE: OUTDOOR LIFE": Outdoor Life says Fraser used a Fenwick rod,
  a Penn International 14/0 reel and 130 lb line while trolling a mackerel. The drawn reel, rod and chair are generic,
  not models of his tackle.
- "Average gear will snap in seconds": script rhetoric, not a measured claim.
- "Troll fresh mackerel or herring" (angler technique): common giant bluefin practice; Fraser's fish took a mackerel
  (Outdoor Life).
- Pins "fighting chair", "roller guides", "130 lb mono", "fresh mackerel", "temperature break": labels of the drawn
  rig. The temperature break is a drawn colour edge, illustrative.

## Beat 3: thermal zone, badge [2] THERMOCLINE DEPTH
- "Giant bluefin track temperature breaks" (angler technique): common offshore advice; not attributed to research.
- "Their dives follow the thermocline" / "TAGGING: WALLI ET AL. 2009, PLOS ONE" / badge line "where their dives
  level off": Walli et al. tagged 561 Atlantic bluefin (1996 to 2005, 106 tags recovered); mean diving depth tracked
  thermocline depth across the North Atlantic, and dives were shallower and more frequent in foraging areas.
  Walli A, Teo SLH, Boustany A, Farwell CJ, Williams T, Dewar H, Prince E, Block BA (2009) Seasonal Movements,
  Aggregations and Diving Behavior of Atlantic Bluefin Tuna (Thunnus thynnus) Revealed with Archival Tags. PLoS ONE
  4(7): e6151. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0006151
  https://swfsc-publications.fisheries.noaa.gov/publications/CR/2009/2009Wal.pdf
- "the boundary between warm surface water and the cold water below" (voiceover) and the pins "warm surface water",
  "thermocline", "cold deep water": the standard definition of a thermocline; the depths drawn are not to scale.
- "They can dive past 3,000 feet" / "TAGGING: BLOCK ET AL. 2001, SCIENCE" / pin "dives past 3,000 ft": Block et al.
  report Atlantic bluefin diving below 1,000 m while keeping a warm body. 1,000 m x 3.281 ft/m = 3,281 ft, so "past
  3,000 feet" holds. Block BA, Dewar H, Blackwell SB, et al. (2001) Migratory movements, depth preferences, and thermal
  biology of Atlantic bluefin tuna. Science 293(5533): 1310 to 1314. doi:10.1126/science.1061197
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11509729/
  The animated dive is short (the shot lasts seconds) and does not reach that depth.

## Beat 4: the fight, badge [3] BACK DOWN HARD
- "The angler doesn't fight alone. The captain backs down hard" / "keep line on the spool" (angler technique):
  backing down on a big fish is standard stand up and chair practice; a Marlin Magazine drag article notes a case
  where a single screw boat could not back up or turn fast enough to keep up with the fish.
  https://www.marlinmag.com/?p=43769
- "Line on the spool. Heavy drag." (angler technique): drag guidance varies. A common rule of thumb is a strike drag
  near 25 to 30 percent of line breaking strength (about 30 to 40 lb on 130 lb line; 0.30 x 130 = 39 lb); a Pakula
  guide suggests fighting drag of roughly 50 to 70 percent of line class for very large fish. The film shows no
  numbers. https://georgepoveromo.com/how_to_manage_your_drag.html
  https://www.pakula.com.au/index.php/btl/0001-between-the-lines-7/setting-the-drag
- "half ton fish" (voiceover): rhetoric for a giant bluefin; Fraser's fish was 1,496 lb, about 0.75 short tons.
- Pins "backing down", "rod doubled over": labels of the simulated action.

## Outro
- "The ultimate apex predator of the sea": script wording, not a scientific classification.

## What is simulated
The whole film is computer generated: the fish, its swimming, the line, the boat and the angler, the sea, the
plankton glow (stylised bioluminescence), the depths, the cross section diagram and the fight. It is not
footage of Fraser's fish or of any real catch.
